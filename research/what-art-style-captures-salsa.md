## Comprehensive Analysis

All three models converge powerfully on one central insight: the art style that best captures salsa must carry the **cultural DNA** of the Afro-Caribbean diaspora, not merely depict bodies in motion. This is a high-confidence finding you can act on. Whether labeled "Latin figurative expressionism," "Afro-Caribbean expressionism," or "Afrocubanismo," the core recommendation is the same: a style that fuses bold, saturated tropical color palettes with stylized figurative forms, rhythmic composition, and emotional intensity rooted in Caribbean identity. Artists like Mario Carreño (*The Afro-Cuban Dance*, 1943), Wifredo Lam, and the contemporary muralist Andres Giraldo all work in this tradition, using exaggerated curves, warm reds and ochres, and simplified facial features that let the *body* tell the story—exactly as salsa itself does.[^1_8][^1_1][^1_11][^1_3][^1_2]

The unanimous rejection of photorealism and the shared skepticism toward Impressionism are equally telling. All models agree that salsa's essence is subjective—passion, improvisation, connection—and that a style capturing it must prioritize feeling over accuracy. Impressionism, while historically linked to dance through Degas's ballet paintings, was deemed too restrained and European in palette to match salsa's tropical fire.[^1_5][^1_6][^1_7][^1_8]

The most interesting divergence lies in **how culturally specific** the recommendation should be. GPT-5.4 Thinking approaches the question primarily through painterly technique—loose brushwork, expressive color, sweeping curves—making its recommendation more universally applicable to any artist's toolkit. Claude Opus 4.7 Thinking and Gemini 3.1 Pro Thinking, by contrast, anchor their answers to specific art-historical movements and name concrete reference points. Claude Opus 4.7 Thinking's identification of Fania Records album art as the literal "visual identity of salsa" is particularly compelling—those 1970s covers by Izzy Sanabria created an iconic bold-outline, high-contrast, community-celebratory aesthetic that most salsa lovers instantly recognize. Gemini 3.1 Pro Thinking's structural argument—that Cubist fragmentation visually replicates the uneven syncopation of the clave beat—adds an intellectually satisfying layer, suggesting that Afro-Cuban Modernism doesn't just *look* like salsa but formally *functions* like it.[^1_2]

Gemini 3.1 Pro Thinking's unique suggestion that **different salsa sub-styles map to different art styles** is worth noting given your knowledge of salsa variations. Art Deco's clean geometry suits the linear slot-work of NY On2 and LA On1 styles, while Fauvism's raw, saturated abandon better captures a chaotic Friday night at La Placita. This nuanced approach could be especially useful for your salsa card game project—different visual styles for different move categories could reinforce the feel of each dance style.[^1_12][^1_13]

For practical application, whether you're commissioning art, choosing prints for your space, or developing visual assets for your card game, start with the **Afro-Caribbean figurative expressionist tradition**: bold outlines, warm saturated color, rhythmic figure repetition, and stylized rather than realistic bodies. Look at Fania album covers, Andres Giraldo's murals, and Carreño's paintings as direct reference points. And when you're in San Juan for La Placita, consider visiting the Santurce arts district nearby—it's a living gallery of exactly this aesthetic.[^1_11][^1_14]
