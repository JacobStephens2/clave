# Salsa Competition Scoring Guide

## Overview

Salsa competitions evaluate dancers across a structured set of criteria covering musicality, technique, partnership, and presentation. While specific weightings vary across events, the underlying framework is consistent across most major salsa congresses and festivals worldwide. Understanding how judges assess performance — and how placements are calculated — can meaningfully inform how a dancer trains and prepares.

***

## Competition Formats

Salsa competitions generally fall into two main categories, each with a distinct judging approach:

- **Showcase / Choreography** — Pre-planned routines performed to a chosen song. Judged on the full package: technique, choreography design, musicality interpretation, and stage performance.
- **Freestyle / Jack & Jill (J&J)** — Improvised dancing. In J&J, partners are randomly assigned, testing pure social dance ability without rehearsal.[^1][^2]

Some events also offer **Pro-Am** divisions, where only the amateur partner is judged and the professional serves solely as a facilitating lead or follow.[^3]

***

## Core Judging Criteria

Most salsa competitions score across five to six core dimensions. The table below reflects typical weights drawn from multiple major festival rulebooks:[^4][^5]

| Criterion | Typical Weight | What Judges Look For |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | 20–30% | Consistent beat accuracy on chosen style (On1, On2, etc.) [^5][^6] |
| **Musicality** | 15–30% | Interpreting dynamics, accents, pauses, and musical phrasing[^6][^7] |
| **Technique** | 15–25% | Footwork quality, body movement, posture, turn execution[^4][^6] |
| **Choreography / Difficulty** | 10–35% | Creativity, variety of moves, appropriate difficulty level[^4][^5] |
| **Connection** | 15–20% | Lead/follow quality and partnership synchronization[^5][^8] |
| **Presentation / Projection** | 5–15% | Stage presence, expression, costume, and styling[^4][^6] |

### Real-World Examples

Scoring percentages differ meaningfully between events, reflecting each competition's values:

- **San Francisco Salsa Festival**: Timing 30% · Presentation/Choreography/Musicality 25% · Difficulty & Technique 25% · Connection & Synchronization 20%[^5]
- **Varna Salsa Festival**: Choreography 35% · Timing/Musicality 30% · Technique 20% · Projection 10% · Costume/Styling 5%[^4]

***

## Showcase-Specific Rules

For choreographed divisions, routines must contain a recognized minimum percentage of salsa-specific content — commonly 70–80% or more. Excessive fusion with other styles results in point deductions, typically around 10% of the total score.[^6][^4]

Competitions also distinguish between:

- **Cabaret** divisions — Tricks, lifts, and acrobatics are permitted
- **Non-Cabaret** divisions — At least one foot must remain on the floor at all times; aerial moves are prohibited[^6]

Costume requirements, minimum routine duration (typically 3–5 minutes), and music submission deadlines are also common rules that can affect scoring or eligibility.[^8][^4]

***

## Freestyle & Jack and Jill Scoring

Freestyle and J&J divisions typically use a **ranking system** rather than a numerical score sheet. Each judge ranks competitors across one or more songs, and those rankings are averaged to determine overall placement.[^8]

For J&J specifically, judges prioritize:

- **Timing** — Staying on a consistent beat throughout the song
- **Partner connection** — Reading leads and follows, adapting to an unknown partner
- **Musicality** — Responding to the song's energy, mood, and structure
- **Enjoyment and attitude** — Genuine engagement, confidence, and social dance etiquette[^2][^9][^1]

Any dancing that appears pre-choreographed in a J&J round — particularly synchronized shine sequences performed together — can result in disqualification. The spirit of J&J is improvisation and real-time connection.[^1]

***

## How Placements Are Determined

Many competitions use the **Relative Placement System** (also called the Skating System), which is majority-based rather than a simple average of scores. The process works as follows:[^10]

1. A competitor wins 1st place if a **majority of judges** rank them first.
2. If no majority exists, judges expand the window to "1st or 2nd," continuing until a majority is reached.
3. If two competitors share the same majority threshold, the tie is broken by counting **how many judges** contributed to that majority.
4. If still tied, the raw sum of placement numbers is compared — the lower total wins.[^10]

Judge panels typically consist of 5–9 judges, with finals requiring an **odd number** to prevent deadlocks. This system prevents a single outlier score from skewing results and rewards consistent excellence across the panel.[^11]

***

## Skill Divisions

Competitors are stratified to ensure fair matchups. Standard divisions include:[^3]

- **Beginner** — Little to no competitive experience
- **Intermediate** — Some experience but not yet competing at advanced level
- **Advanced** — Strong technical foundation, competition-experienced
- **Professional / Open** — Top-level competitive and performing dancers

Some congresses further split divisions by style (**On1 vs. On2**) to avoid judging incompatible timing systems against each other. In Pro-Am categories, the professional partner's performance is not evaluated — judging focuses entirely on the amateur's growth and execution.[^3]

***

## Key Takeaways for Competitors

- **Timing is always weighted heavily** — dancing cleanly on beat often accounts for the largest single scoring block.[^5]
- **Musicality elevates scores** — dancers who visibly respond to the music's texture (not just its beat) consistently score higher than those who only hit the downbeats.[^7][^6]
- **Connection wins J&J** — since a partner is assigned at random, the ability to adapt, listen, and communicate through movement is the most differentiating skill in that format.[^2][^1]
- **Consistency across judges matters more than one great score** — the Relative Placement System rewards median excellence over a single standout ranking.[^10]
