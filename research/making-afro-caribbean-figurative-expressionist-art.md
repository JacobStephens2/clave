### 4. Comprehensive Analysis

**High-Confidence Findings**
All three models converge on the idea that “Afro-Caribbean figurative expressionism” is best approached as a broad, living set of related practices rather than a single codified movement. That matters because it frees you to build a personal visual language while still staying in the tradition’s gravitational field: the figure as primary meaning-maker, emotional distortion as truth-telling, and a creolized mixing of sources (African diasporic, Indigenous Caribbean, European modernism) rather than a neat stylistic recipe.[^1_1][^1_4][^1_2]

They also agree that the **body** is the main site where the work’s stakes become visible—history, spirituality, migration, labor, intimacy, violence, survival. In practical terms: begin with a strong pose/silhouette, then push anatomy intentionally (elongation, compression, fragmentation) to express pressure, ecstasy, mourning, or transformation rather than optical correctness. This is reinforced by widely cited models like Wifredo Lam, whose return to the Caribbean drove him to merge physical, spiritual, and political realms through hybrid figures and dense vegetal space.[^1_4][^1_2][^1_3][^1_8][^1_1]

Another solid consensus is the importance of **hybridity** and **figure–environment fusion**: people become cane, mangrove, waves, birds; land and spirit enter the body and vice versa. This isn’t just an aesthetic trick; it’s a worldview move—refusing the colonial separation of “nature vs. culture” and insisting on ancestral presence in ecology and everyday life. Alongside that, all models emphasize that color and surface rhythm are often bold and dense, but most compelling when **symbolic** and structurally rhythmic rather than merely “tropical.”[^1_5][^1_2][^1_3][^1_4]

**Areas of Divergence**
Where they split is mainly *how* to operationalize the tradition. Claude Opus 4.7 encourages more explicit engagement with tradition-specific iconographies (orisha attributes, Vodou vèvè, etc.) and even stresses correctness as respect. GPT-5.4 Thinking shares the respect concern but is more cautious: it urges you to treat this as a tradition of relation and to avoid pulling sacred imagery as decorative mood-board material, pointing to Manuel Mendive as a model of practice-based immersion rather than surface borrowing. Gemini 3.1 Pro Thinking is the most direct about using religious symbols as living forces in the painting, which can be powerful—yet also increases the ethical burden to research and consult appropriately.[^1_6][^1_5]

They also diverge by emphasizing different “entry ramps.” GPT-5.4’s path is compositional and conceptual (build an image-world; pick symbolic anchors; fuse body/land; make marks feel like sound), which is a reliable approach if you want coherence without over-dependence on specific icon sets. Claude’s path is lineage-and-vocabulary driven: study artists, adopt a symbolic toolkit, then orchestrate pattern/ornament as political and spiritual charge. Gemini’s path is materially driven—thick build-up, scraping back, frottage imprinting—making the surface itself a metaphor for layered history.[^1_9][^1_3][^1_8][^1_5]

**Unique Insights Worth Noting**
Gemini’s “excavation” idea is particularly usable: build bright strata, cover with darker layers, then scratch/reveal so the painting literally embodies concealment, survival, and return. Even if you don’t adopt Roche Rabell’s exact method, the concept of *revealed underlayers* translates well to acrylic, oil, collage, and mixed media. GPT-5.4’s “Icon + wound + rhythm + transformation” is also a strong quality-control test: if one is missing, you can diagnose why a piece is becoming poster-like, purely tragic, merely decorative, or too abstracted from lived stakes.[^1_9]

**Recommendations**
Start by choosing one island/diaspora context and one lived theme (migration, devotion, carnival, labor, mourning, sea-memory), then build a single-figure or small-group composition where the body merges with one ecological force (cane/mangrove/wave) and includes 2–3 symbolic anchors you truly understand. Use bold color *with assigned meaning*, and consider an “excavated surface” workflow (layer → cover → scrape/reveal) to embed history physically into the painting. If you plan to reference Vodou/Santería/Lukumí/Abakuá imagery, do the research first and—ideally—consult practitioners so the work stays in relation, not extraction.[^1_2][^1_3][^1_4][^1_5][^1_6][^1_9]
